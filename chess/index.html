Thinking in chess

Thinking in chess

Strategy and tactics from beginner to club player: how to find good moves, avoid bad ones, and build an advantage one step at a time.

You know how the pieces move. You've probably fallen for a trap or two and set a few of your own. What's missing is the part nobody explains well: how do strong players decide what to do? Why castle? What does "control the center" actually buy you? When is a trade good? How do you find a plan when nothing is happening?

This guide answers those questions in the order that matters for improving. That order surprises people. At the beginner and early intermediate level, games are decided overwhelmingly by tactics: a piece left hanging, a fork not seen, a mate in two missed. The old line attributed to Richard Teichmann, that chess is 99 percent tactics, overstates it, but not by as much as you'd hope. So Part I is about a thinking routine that stops you from losing material, Part II is about the tactical patterns that win it, and only then do we get to openings, plans, pawn structure and endgames.

Part I

Seeing the board

A routine for every move, what the pieces are worth, and how to count before you capture.

1Reading the diagrams

The diagrams use algebraic notation. Each square is named by its file (the column, a to h, left to right from White's side) and its rank (the row, 1 to 8, from White's side). Pieces are abbreviated K, Q, R, B and N (knight, since K is taken). Pawns get no letter: "e4" means a pawn moved to e4. An "x" means a capture, "+" means check, "#" means checkmate, and O-O and O-O-O are kingside and queenside castling.

Moves are numbered in pairs: "1.e4 e5" is White's first move and Black's reply. When a line starts with Black's move, it's written with an ellipsis, like "6… Ke7". You'll also see the standard annotation marks: ! for a strong move, !! for a brilliant one, ? for a mistake and ?? for a blunder.

Throughout, the board is shown from White's side. When a position is about Black's play, the caption says so.

2The move-by-move routine

The single biggest jump from beginner to intermediate isn't knowledge. It's process. Chess coach Dan Heisman gave the most common beginner habit a name: hope chess. You make a move without checking whether your opponent has a reply you can't meet, and hope they don't find it. Its opposite, which he calls real chess, is simply doing that check every single move. It sounds obvious. Almost nobody does it consistently until they train themselves to.

Here's a real trap that punishes hope chess. Black wins White's queen on move 5 and is checkmated two moves later.

Black saw "I can take a queen" and stopped thinking. The fix is a fixed sequence of questions, asked in the same order every move until it becomes automatic.

Step 1: What did that move do?

When your opponent moves, before you think about your own plans, ask what their move changed. What does the moved piece attack now? What did it stop defending? What line did it open behind it? Most blunders happen because a player keeps thinking about their own idea and treats the opponent's move as noise.

Step 2: Checks, captures, threats

Look at every check, every capture and every threat available, for both sides. These are called forcing moves, because they limit the opponent's replies. Forcing moves are where tactics live. Check them in that order, and don't skip the silly-looking ones: the queen sacrifice that "obviously" doesn't work is exactly the one you need to rule out properly.

Step 3: Candidate moves

List two or three reasonable moves. If there's nothing forcing, candidates come from the position's needs: develop a piece, improve your worst piece, attack a weakness, prevent the opponent's idea (Part IV covers how to find these).

Step 4: Blunder check

Before you touch the piece, imagine the board after your move and ask one question: what checks, captures and threats does my opponent have now? This is the step that stops hope chess. It takes a few seconds and prevents most lost games at club level.

Why this beats studying openings

A player who never hangs pieces and always notices a two-move threat will beat a player who knows twenty moves of theory but plays hope chess. You'll see this routine behind every puzzle in this guide. If you take one thing from it, take this.

A caution from the other direction: Dutch International Master Willy Hendriks has argued that beginner thinking systems can become rituals. Strong players mostly recognize patterns rather than working through checklists. Both things are true. The checklist is how you learn to see patterns until you no longer need it, the way you once sounded out words before you could read.

3What pieces are worth

You need a common currency to judge trades. The traditional scale is measured in pawns:

PieceClassic valueRefinement
Pawn1Central pawns are worth a bit more than edge pawns; a pawn close to promotion can be worth far more.
Knight3Better in closed positions with many pawns.
Bishop3Better in open positions. Two bishops together are worth about half a pawn extra.
Rook5Weak in the opening, dominant in the endgame.
Queen9Roughly two rooks, or three minor pieces, depending on the position.
King—Priceless, but in the endgame fights like a piece worth about 3–4.

Those refinements come mostly from IM (now GM) Larry Kaufman's study of hundreds of thousands of master games. His key findings: a single bishop and a single knight are worth the same on average, so which is better depends on the position; the bishop pair is worth about half a pawn, enough to matter; and a rook's pawn (a- or h-file) is worth a bit less than other pawns, since it can only capture in one direction.

The numbers are averages. A piece's real value is what it can do right now, and that depends on where it stands.

This is the seed of all positional chess. If two armies are equal in material, the one whose pieces control more squares, and more important squares, is better. Every later idea (development, outposts, open files, good and bad bishops) is a way of making your pieces worth more than their sticker price and your opponent's worth less.

Common trades

Some exchanges come up constantly. Two minor pieces for a rook and pawn is usually good for the side with the two pieces in the middlegame, since pieces coordinate and rooks need open files. Giving a rook for a minor piece is called losing "the exchange"; it's a two-point loss on paper and a real one most of the time. Three minor pieces for a queen is usually fine for the pieces. A queen for two rooks is about equal.

4Counting and trading

When pieces pile up on a square, you can work out what happens without calculating every move. Count attackers and defenders of the target. If you have more attackers, you can usually win it. Then check the order: capture with your least valuable pieces first, because the side that captures last on the square is the one that keeps material.

Two refinements make counting accurate. First, values matter, not just numbers: if your only attacker is a queen and their defender is a pawn, "one against one" still means you lose the queen. Second, either side can stop an exchange sequence whenever continuing would lose material, so compare where the sequence ends at each point, not only at the end.

When to trade

Beginners either trade everything to simplify or avoid all trades out of fear. Both are ways of not thinking. Trades are tools with predictable effects:

SituationGuidelineWhy
You're ahead in materialTrade pieces, not pawnsAn extra piece matters more as the board empties; keep pawns so you have something to promote.
You're behindAvoid piece trades, trade pawnsComplications are your friend; fewer pawns means more drawing chances.
You're under attackTrade the attacking piecesEach trade removes an attacker; attacks die when the attackers are gone.
You're attackingAvoid trades, especially of your best attackerKeep the pressure; the defender wants trades.
You have a bad pieceTrade it for their good pieceA bishop blocked by its own pawns for their active knight is a bargain.
Space advantageAvoid tradesThe cramped side wants trades to breathe.
CrampedSeek tradesFewer pieces need less room.

Before any trade, ask: after this trade, which remaining pieces are better, mine or theirs? And whose pawn structure improves? A trade that looks equal on the value scale can be a clear gain or loss once you ask that.

Part II

Tactics

Short, forcing sequences that win material or mate. Recognizing these patterns is the fastest way to raise your rating.

5Loose pieces and forcing moves

Grandmaster John Nunn coined a four-letter warning: LPDO, loose pieces drop off. A loose piece is one that isn't defended. Most tactics are aimed at loose pieces or at the king, because those are the targets that can't be protected by a simple recapture. So before you look for fancy combinations, just scan the board: which pieces are undefended, mine and theirs? Which pieces are defended only once while being attacked?

Tactics work because of forcing moves. A check has to be answered. A capture usually has to be recaptured. A threat to a queen or to mate has to be met. When your opponent has only one or two legal or sensible replies, you can calculate much further ahead, because the tree of possibilities barely branches. That's why good players calculate forcing lines first and quiet moves last.

Almost every tactic is a double attack at heart: one move creates two threats, and the opponent can only answer one. The next chapters are different ways to create that.

The in-between move

In an exchange sequence, the "obvious" recapture isn't forced if you have something stronger first, like a check or a bigger threat. This is called a zwischenzug (German for in-between move). Always ask, before recapturing, whether there's a forcing move to play first.

6Forks

A fork is one piece attacking two or more targets at once. Any piece can fork, but the knight is the specialist: its jump can't be blocked, and beginners find its geometry hardest to see. Train yourself to notice when the enemy king and queen, or king and rook, are a knight's jump apart from the same square.

The best forks include a check, because the opponent must spend their move on the king. When you see two enemy pieces that are both loose, ask which of your pieces could hit both, and whether a square exists where it can.

7Pins and skewers

A pin attacks a piece that can't move (or shouldn't) because a more valuable piece stands behind it on the same line. Only long-range pieces pin: bishops, rooks and queens. When the piece behind is the king, the pin is absolute and moving the pinned piece is illegal. Otherwise it's relative: legal, but costly.

Pinned pieces are half-dead. They can't defend what they appear to defend, which is behind many "impossible" captures. The standard ways to exploit a pin: attack the pinned piece again (especially with a pawn), or pile more attackers on it than can defend it. The standard ways to escape one: put a piece between (unpinning, like ...Be7 in the Queen's Gambit), move the piece behind it, or challenge the pinning piece with ...h6 or ...a6.

A skewer reverses the pin: the valuable piece is in front. It has to move, and it exposes what's behind.

8Discovered attacks and double check

A discovered attack happens when one piece moves out of the way of another, unveiling an attack from the piece behind. The moving piece gets a free move to make its own threat. Two threats from one move: a double attack again. When the unveiled attack is a check, it's a discovered check, and it's among the most powerful weapons in chess.

If the moving piece also gives check, it's double check. The only defense to double check is moving the king. You can't block two lines at once, and you can't capture two checking pieces with one move. Puzzle 4 in Part VI shows why that matters.

To spot these, look for your pieces lined up behind your own pieces, aimed at something valuable. That setup is a loaded weapon, and it's also something to watch for when your opponent builds it.

9Removing the defender, overloading and deflection

If a target is defended, attack the defense. There are three closely related versions:

  • Removing the defender: capture or drive away the piece that guards something important.
  • Overloading: one piece is defending two things; make it choose.
  • Deflection: force a defender to move away from its post, usually with a sacrifice it has to take.

The opposite trick is the decoy: lure an enemy piece (often the king) onto a square where a tactic works, like the queen sacrifice in the smothered mate below. When you see a near-miss tactic ("if only that knight weren't there"), ask how to remove, overload, deflect or decoy the obstacle.

10Mating patterns

Checkmates aren't calculated from scratch; they're recognized. Strong players know dozens of patterns, and spotting the first move of one is usually the hard part. Start with these.

The back-rank mate teaches a defensive habit as much as an attacking one. Once the middlegame starts and your back rank has few defenders, find a quiet moment to make luft with h3 or g3 (…h6 or …g6 as Black). Don't do it while pieces are still undeveloped, and don't do it when it weakens squares near your king that the opponent can use right away.

Other patterns worth learning next, all easily found in puzzle trainers by name: Anastasia's mate, the Arabian mate, Boden's mate, the Greek gift sacrifice (Bxh7+ followed by Ng5+ and Qh5), and the two-bishops mate on a castled king.

Part III

The opening

Principles over memorization: what the first ten moves are for, and a small, sound repertoire.

11Three jobs: center, development, king safety

The opening has three jobs, and nearly every rule you've heard is a consequence of them.

Control the center. The four center squares (d4, e4, d5, e5) and the ring around them are where pieces have the most reach, as the knight diagram in Chapter 3 showed. Pieces in the center can switch to either wing quickly; pieces on the edge can't. Controlling the center doesn't necessarily mean occupying it with pawns. It means your pawns and pieces attack those squares, so your pieces can use them and your opponent's can't.

Develop your pieces. A piece on its starting square does nothing. Development means bringing knights and bishops out to active squares where they influence the center, then connecting your rooks. Each opening move is a unit of time, called a tempo. Spending tempi on moves that don't develop (moving a piece twice, early pawn moves on the edge, early queen trips) hands the opponent a lead in development. And a lead in development is a temporary advantage: it only pays off if you open the position and use it before the opponent catches up.

Keep your king safe. The center is where the fighting happens, which makes it a bad place for the king. Castling moves it to the side behind a wall of pawns and brings a rook toward the middle. More in the next chapter.

A good opening move does more than one thing

1.e4 claims the center and opens lines for the queen and bishop. Nf3 develops and attacks e5 and prepares castling. When choosing between moves in the opening, favor the one that serves more of the three jobs.

12Castling

Castling is the only move that develops a rook and secures the king at once. Usually you should castle within the first ten moves or so, and kingside castling (O-O) is the default because it takes fewer preparatory moves.

The danger of skipping it is concrete. The f7 square (f2 for White) is the weak point at the start: only the king defends it. And the center files open quickly once pawns are exchanged, putting an uncastled king directly on the line of fire.

A few practical rules about castling:

  • Don't move the pawns in front of your castled king without a reason. Each pawn move there creates squares the enemy can use and lines they can open. The three pawns f2, g2, h2 (f7, g7, h7) are the king's shelter.
  • Castle long (O-O-O) with a purpose. It's a bit less safe (the a-pawn is unguarded and the king takes an extra move to get to b1), but it puts the rook on the d-file immediately and often signals an attack: see Chapter 18.
  • Sometimes delaying is right. If the center is locked by pawns, the king can wait. If the opponent is planning a pawn storm on one side, it can pay to see which side before committing. In the endgame, don't castle at all; the king should come to the center.
  • The rules. You can't castle out of check, through a square attacked by the enemy, or into check, and not if the king or that rook has moved. You can castle if the rook is attacked or if it passes over an attacked square (relevant for O-O-O and the b-file).

13Opening rules, their exceptions, and traps

The classic opening rules are shortcuts for the three jobs:

  • Start with a center pawn (e4 or d4; …e5 or …d5).
  • Develop knights before bishops, since you usually know where knights belong (f3 and c3) before you know which diagonal the bishops want.
  • Don't move the same piece twice in the opening without a reason.
  • Don't bring the queen out early; she gets chased, and every chase gains the opponent a free developing move.
  • Castle early.
  • Connect your rooks (clear the back rank between them), then put them on open or central files.
  • Don't grab pawns if it costs a lot of development, especially with the queen.

Every one of these has exceptions, and the exceptions have the same shape: a concrete tactical reason beats a general rule. In the Fried Liver line above, White moves the knight twice because Ng5 attacks f7 right now. In the Légal trap in Chapter 2, White "loses" the queen because it leads to mate. General principles tell you what to play when nothing concrete is happening. When something is, calculate.

14A starter repertoire

You don't need much opening theory below intermediate level. You need openings that lead to positions where the principles work and that you understand. Pick one plan with White and one reply as Black to each of 1.e4 and 1.d4, and learn the ideas, not long move lists. Here's a sound, principled set.

With White: 1.e4 and the Italian Game

The ideas: pieces to natural squares, castle, then decide between d3–d4 when well prepared, or maneuvering the b1 knight via d2 and f1 to g3 for a kingside push. If Black plays 2…Nf6 or 2…d6 instead, just keep developing: Bc4, Nc3 or c3, d3 or d4, castle.

With Black against 1.e4: 1…e5

Mirror the same principles. Against the Italian, 3…Bc5 or 3…Nf6 both lead to healthy positions. Against the Ruy Lopez (3.Bb5), 3…a6 and …Nf6 with ...Be7 and ...O-O is time-tested. Know the Fried Liver trap from Chapter 12: after 3…Nf6 4.Ng5 d5 5.exd5, avoid 5…Nxd5 at your level and play 5…Na5 instead.

With Black against 1.d4: the Queen's Gambit Declined

Solid, logical, and it teaches you pawn structure (Chapter 17 and the Carlsbad plan in Chapter 21). If White plays the London System (Bf4, e3, c3, often without c4), the same …d5, …Nf6, …e6, …c5 setup works fine.

Everything else

Against unusual first moves, take the center with a pawn, develop, castle. You'll be fine. Once you're comfortable, look up the first few moves of what opponents actually play against you. Your own game history is the best opening book you'll ever have.

How much theory?

A reasonable rule until you're a solid intermediate player: know the first five to eight moves of your main lines and, more important, why each move is played. If you find yourself out of your preparation on move 6, you're doing it right. Time spent memorizing move 15 is time not spent on tactics and endgames.

Part IV

The middlegame

Evaluating positions, improving pieces, reading pawn structure, applying pressure and making plans.

15Evaluating a position

When the opening is over and there's no tactic, what do you do? You look for differences between the two sides and play to make the ones in your favor matter. International Master Jeremy Silman made this idea popular under the name imbalances. His list, lightly paraphrased:

ImbalanceQuestion to ask
MaterialWho's ahead, and does the extra material matter here?
Minor piecesBishop vs knight: open or closed position? Good or bad bishop? Does anyone have the bishop pair?
Pawn structureIsolated, doubled, backward, passed pawns? Where are the pawn breaks?
SpaceWhose pawns are further advanced, giving their pieces more room?
FilesIs there an open file, and who controls it?
Weak squaresAre there holes (squares no pawn can guard) that a piece could occupy?
DevelopmentWho has more pieces in play?
InitiativeWho's making threats and who's reacting?
King safetyWhose king is more exposed?
Statics vs dynamicsAre the advantages long-term (structure, bishop pair) or short-term (development, initiative)?

That last row is the most important strategic idea in chess. Static advantages are permanent-ish: a better pawn structure, an extra pawn, a knight on an outpost. They favor slow play, trades and endgames. Dynamic advantages are temporary: a lead in development, an initiative, active pieces. They must be used now, usually by opening the position and attacking, or they evaporate. A huge amount of good chess is simply knowing which kind you have and acting accordingly. The Opera Game in Part VI is dynamics in their purest form.

Tactics first, always

Imbalances tell you what to aim for. They don't excuse skipping Chapter 2. Players who start thinking strategically often begin hanging pieces, because they're thinking about outposts instead of checks. Run the checks-captures-threats scan first, every move, and only then ask what the position wants.

16Piece activity

If you remember one middlegame rule, make it this one: find your worst piece and improve it. When there's no tactic and no obvious plan, the piece doing the least is where the next move should come from. This works absurdly well, because a position is only as strong as its weakest link.

Knights

Knights are short-range, so they need forward posts. An outpost is a square in the opponent's half that your pawn protects and no enemy pawn can attack. A knight on an outpost can often be worth more than a rook. When you see a hole in the enemy's pawn structure, ask how to get a knight there.

Bishops

A bishop is good when its own pawns are on the opposite color, leaving its diagonals clear, and bad when its own pawns block it. The fix: put your pawns on the color your remaining bishop doesn't use, or trade the bad bishop. Bishops love open positions and long diagonals, and the bishop pair becomes powerful when the position opens.

Rooks

Rooks need open files (no pawns) or half-open files (only enemy pawns). Put them there, double them (two rooks on one file), then aim for the 7th rank. A rook on the 7th attacks pawns from the side where they can't defend each other and traps the enemy king on the back rank.

The queen

The queen is strong but vulnerable: any lesser piece can chase her with gain of tempo. In the middlegame, she's often best a step behind the action, supporting other pieces, rather than leading it.

17Pawn structure

Pawns are the skeleton of the position. They can't move backward, so every pawn move permanently changes which squares can be guarded. Pieces come and go, but the pawn structure tells you where the pieces belong and what the long-term plans are.

A few principles fall out of these pictures:

  • Every pawn move creates a weakness (the squares it can no longer guard). That's not a reason never to move pawns. It's a reason to move them with purpose.
  • Weaknesses are targets only if you can attack them. A backward pawn on a half-open file, attacked by rooks, is a real problem; one that no piece can reach is cosmetic.
  • Pawn breaks are how plans happen. A pawn break is a pawn move that challenges the enemy pawns to open lines: …c5 and …f6 in the French, d4 in the Italian, b4–b5 in the Carlsbad. When you're unsure what to do, ask which pawn breaks are available to each side, and whether you can prepare yours or prevent theirs.
  • Capture toward the center. When recapturing with a pawn, taking toward the middle usually improves your structure.
  • Passed pawns must be pushed, and they're best blockaded by a knight, which isn't blinded by the pawn in front of it.

18Space, the center and where to attack

Space is the territory behind your pawn chain. More space means more room for your pieces to maneuver and less for theirs. The side with less space should look for trades (fewer pieces need less room) and pawn breaks to free themselves. The side with more space should avoid trades and keep the bind.

Where to act depends on the center:

  • Open center (few central pawns): pieces rule, the uncastled king is in danger, and wing attacks are risky because the opponent can counter through the middle.
  • Closed center (locked pawn chains): play slows down and moves to the wings. Attack on the side where your pawn chain points, since that's where you have more space.
  • Fluid center: whoever controls the central pawn breaks controls the game.

The classic principle: a wing attack is best answered by a counterattack in the center. If your opponent throws pawns at your king while the center can still open, open it.

With kings on opposite sides, pawn storms become the main weapon, because advancing pawns toward the enemy king doesn't expose your own. These positions are races. Count tempi: how many moves until your attack breaks through, and how many for theirs? Defensive moves that don't slow the opponent by more than they cost you usually lose the race.

19Initiative and pressure

In chess, pressure means creating threats that force your opponent to spend moves reacting. The side making threats has the initiative, and holding it is often worth more than a pawn, because a defender who is always responding never gets to execute their own plan, and eventually runs out of good defenses.

How to build pressure

  • Fix a target, then pile up. Find a weakness that can't move (a backward pawn, a pinned piece, a weak square). Aim more pieces at it than can defend it. Doubled rooks and a queen on a half-open file against a backward pawn is the textbook picture.
  • Make threats that improve your position. A developing move with a threat gains a tempo; a threat that forces a piece to a worse square gains even more.
  • Tie pieces down. Every enemy piece stuck defending is a piece not attacking you. The rook on the 7th in Chapter 16 tied Black's rook to b7.
  • Use the principle of two weaknesses. One weakness can usually be defended. So create a second one on the other side of the board and switch between them. Defenders can't be in two places; the position cracks where they aren't.

Prophylaxis

The other half of pressure is stopping theirs. Prophylaxis means asking "what does my opponent want to do?" and preventing it before it happens. If your opponent's best piece wants a certain square, take the square away. If their pawn break is their only counterplay, stop the break. Strong players spend a large fraction of their moves this way. It looks passive and is anything but: a player with no active plan tends to make weakening moves.

20Attacking and defending the king

Attacks on the king succeed for measurable reasons. Before you launch one, check the conditions:

  • More attackers than defenders near the king. Count pieces within reach of the king's area, not just on the board.
  • Open lines toward the king, or pawns you can use to open them (a pawn storm, a sacrifice on h7 or g6, a pawn break).
  • A stable or closed center, so the opponent can't counterattack in the middle.
  • Weaknesses in the pawn shelter: pawns already moved to h6, g6 or f6 give hooks to attack and holes to use.

If most of those are present, attack and look for sacrifices; the defender's pieces won't arrive in time. If they aren't, the attack is probably premature, and you should improve pieces first.

Defending

Defense is a skill. The principles: trade the attacking pieces (especially the enemy queen), bring defenders toward the king, don't create new pawn weaknesses in the shelter unless forced, counterattack in the center, and when you're in real trouble, give material back to break the attack. A pawn or even the exchange returned at the right moment often ends an attack for good.

21Making a plan

A plan is just an answer to "what should my pieces and pawns be doing over the next several moves?" You don't need to be deep. You need to be consistent. Here's a process that works:

  1. Safety scan. Checks, captures, threats (Chapter 2). If something's hanging or there's a tactic, deal with it.
  2. Imbalances. What are the differences between the sides? Which are static, which dynamic?
  3. Targets. What weakness in their camp could I attack? What's the weakest point in mine?
  4. The ideal setup. Where would each of my pieces stand in a perfect world to use my advantages or hit their weaknesses? Which pawn break supports that?
  5. The worst piece. Which of my pieces is furthest from its ideal square? Move it.
  6. Their plan. What do they want? Can I stop it cheaply?

Here's the process on a classic structure that arises from the Queen's Gambit Declined when White exchanges on d5.

Walking through the process: no tactics. The imbalance is structure: Black has three queenside pawns to White's two, and a half-open e-file; White has a half-open c-file. White's target is the c6/d5 pawn pair. White's ideal setup: rooks on b1 and c1, pawns on b4–b5 to exchange on c6. That leaves Black with either an isolated d5 pawn or a backward c6 pawn: a static weakness White can pile up on (Chapter 19). Black's plan is the opposite wing: pieces toward White's king, perhaps a knight to e4. Both sides now know what to do for the next fifteen moves. That's a plan.

Part V

The endgame

Fewer pieces, different rules. A little endgame knowledge wins a surprising number of games.

22The endgame mindset

When the queens come off and a handful of pieces remain, several middlegame rules flip:

  • The king becomes a fighting piece. With little danger of mate, bring it toward the center and toward the pawns. A centralized king is often worth a whole piece in a pawn ending.
  • Passed pawns become the main currency. Creating one, pushing it, and stopping theirs is what most endgames are about.
  • Rooks belong behind passed pawns, yours or theirs (Tarrasch's rule). Behind your own, the rook gains power as the pawn advances; behind theirs, it stays useful as the pawn advances.
  • Don't rush. In a winning endgame, there's usually no hurry. Improve everything first (king, rook, pawn structure), then break through. Hurried pushes let the defender set up a fortress.
  • Trade pieces when ahead (Chapter 4), and remember that the defender's best hope is often trading all the pawns.

Endgame study also improves your middlegame: once you know which endings are won, you know which trades to go for.

23King and pawn endings

King and pawn endings are pure calculation, and they turn on two ideas. The first is opposition: kings standing on the same file (or rank) with one square between them. The side not to move holds the opposition, because the other king has to give way. The second is key squares: squares that guarantee promotion if the attacking king reaches them. For a pawn that hasn't crossed the middle of the board, they're the three squares two ranks in front of it.

The takeaways: with king and pawn against king, the attacking king should lead the pawn, not follow it. The defender should stay in front of the pawn and take the opposition whenever possible. And the whole outcome can hinge on whose turn it is, which is why endgame players count moves so carefully.

When the defending king is far away, a simpler tool applies: the rule of the square.

24Basic checkmates

You must be able to mate with king and queen, and with king and rook, quickly and without stalemate. Both follow the same method: use the queen or rook to cut the enemy king off into a shrinking box, bring your own king up to support, and push the enemy king to the edge. With a rook, the final picture is kings facing each other with the rook checking along the edge.

With a queen, the danger is stalemate (see the Stalemate tab in Chapter 10). A safe method: put the queen a knight's move away from the enemy king and shadow it as it moves, which pushes it to the edge; then stop and bring your own king over before delivering mate. Practice both against a computer until you can do them in under a minute. It's also worth knowing that king and two knights can't force mate, and that king and bishop and knight can, though that one is rare enough to learn later.

25Rook endings

Rook endings are the most common endings in practice, so two positions are worth knowing by name. The Lucena position is the standard win: the attacking king is on the promotion square with its pawn on the 7th, and it needs shelter from checks.

The Philidor position is the standard draw: the defending king stands in front of the pawn and the rook guards the 6th rank until the pawn advances; then the rook drops back and checks from behind.

Beyond those two, remember: active rooks beat passive ones, even at the cost of a pawn. A rook cutting off the enemy king is worth a lot. And the old saying that all rook endings are drawn exaggerates, but the defending side really does hold more often than beginners expect, so don't give up in them.

Part VI

Putting it together

A famous game, practical habits, a training plan, and puzzles to test yourself.

26The Opera Game

In 1858, the American Paul Morphy played this game during a performance at the Paris opera against two strong amateurs consulting together. It's the most-taught game in chess because it shows, in 17 moves, what a lead in development does against a king left in the center. Step through it and watch for the ideas from earlier chapters: pins, the weak f7 square, development over pawn-grabbing, sacrifices to open lines, removing the defender, and the back-rank mate.

Notice what Morphy didn't do. He never grabbed material when development was available (7.Qb3 attacks b7, but after 7…Qe7 he played 8.Nc3 rather than take). He sacrificed a knight, a rook and his queen, all to keep lines open while Black's kingside pieces sat at home. That's the principle from Chapter 15: a dynamic advantage must be used before it disappears.

27Practical play

The clock

Use your time where it matters. Spend it on critical moments: when the position changes character, when captures start, when a plan must be chosen. Move faster on obvious recaptures and developing moves. And never, ever skip the blunder check to save time. A bad move played fast is still a bad move.

When you're winning

Most won positions are lost through relaxation. Keep running the routine. Trade pieces, stop counterplay, take no unnecessary risks, and ask each move: what is my opponent's only hope? Take that hope away.

When you're losing

Don't resign early at the club level; opponents miss things constantly. Make the position complicated: keep pieces on, create threats, look for stalemate tricks and perpetual check, set problems that require precise calculation. Games are decided by the last mistake, not the first.

After the game

This is where improvement actually happens. Before you turn on an engine, go through the game yourself and note where you felt unsure, where the evaluation swung, and what you were thinking. Then check with the engine. The goal isn't to learn the engine's best move; it's to find why you missed it. "I didn't see his bishop's diagonal" is fixable. "The engine says +0.3" is not a lesson.

28A training plan

Improvement comes from a balance of playing, solving and reviewing. Rating numbers vary a lot between sites and federations, so these stages are described by skills rather than ratings.

Stage 1: stop losing material

You're here if most of your losses involve a piece left hanging or a tactic you didn't see coming. Work on: the Chapter 2 routine every single move; daily tactics puzzles focused on basic patterns (forks, pins, skewers, back rank, mate in 1 and 2); K+Q and K+R mates; the opening principles. Play games of 15 minutes plus 10 seconds per move or slower, so you have time to actually run the routine.

Stage 2: win material, then win the game

You're here if you rarely hang pieces outright, but lose to combinations two or three moves deep, or win material and then fail to convert. Work on: tactics at greater volume and depth (combinations of three to five moves); King and pawn endings and the Lucena and Philidor; a small repertoire you understand (Chapter 14); the "worst piece" rule and simple plans; reviewing every loss.

Stage 3: play positions, not just moves

You're here if tactics decide fewer of your games and you're outplayed slowly instead. Work on: pawn structures and their standard plans (Chapter 17); annotated master games, where a strong player explains every move; calculation practice (set up a position, calculate without moving pieces, then check); deeper rook endgames; building your openings from your own games.

A weekly shape

A reasonable week for someone with an hour a day: most days, 20–30 minutes of puzzles plus one longer game with a short review. Once or twice a week, swap puzzles for endgame study or one annotated master game. Playing lots of blitz feels like practice but trains habits of moving without checking. Treat it as recreation.

For the tinkerer

If you like building your own tools, the python-chess library plus a local Stockfish can scan your games for the moments that mattered. Export your games from Lichess (its API serves a user's games as PGN at lichess.org/api/games/user/USERNAME) and flag every move where the evaluation swung by two pawns or more. Filter to your own color and you have a personal list of missed tactics to review.

import chess.pgn, chess.engine

eng = chess.engine.SimpleEngine.popen_uci("stockfish")
with open("games.pgn") as f:
    while (game := chess.pgn.read_game(f)):
        board, prev = game.board(), None
        for node in game.mainline():
            board.push(node.move)
            info = eng.analyse(board, chess.engine.Limit(depth=14))
            s = info["score"].white().score(mate_score=10000)
            if prev is not None and abs(s - prev) >= 200:
                print(game.headers["Site"], node.san(), prev, "->", s)
            prev = s
eng.quit()

  2. Checks, captures, threats (both sides)

31Glossary

Absolute pin
A pin against the king; the pinned piece cannot legally move off the line.
Back-rank mate
Checkmate by a rook or queen on the first or eighth rank against a king trapped by its own pawns.
Backward pawn
A pawn whose neighbors have advanced past it and that can't safely advance itself.
Bad bishop
A bishop hemmed in by its own pawns on its color.
Bishop pair
Having both bishops when the opponent doesn't; worth about half a pawn on average.
Blockade
Placing a piece directly in front of an enemy passed pawn to stop it. Knights are ideal blockaders.
Blunder check
Imagining the position after your intended move and looking for the opponent's checks, captures and threats.
Candidate moves
The short list of reasonable moves you actually consider.
Castling
Moving king two squares toward a rook and the rook to the king's other side, in one move.
Center
The squares d4, e4, d5 and e5, plus the ring of squares around them.
Connected rooks
Rooks on the same rank with nothing between them, so they defend each other.
Counterplay
Active threats by the side that's worse or under attack.
Decoy
Luring an enemy piece to a square where a tactic works.
Deflection
Forcing a defender away from what it protects.
Development
Bringing pieces off their starting squares to useful posts.
Discovered attack
Moving a piece to unveil an attack by another piece behind it.
Double check
Check from two pieces at once; only a king move answers it.
Doubled pawns
Two pawns of one color on the same file.
Dynamic advantage
A temporary edge, like development or initiative, that must be used quickly.
Exchange, the
Rook for a minor piece. "Winning the exchange" means getting the rook.
Fianchetto
Developing a bishop to b2, g2, b7 or g7 after moving the knight's pawn one square.
File
A column of squares, a through h. Open: no pawns. Half-open: only enemy pawns.
Fork
One piece attacking two or more targets at once.
Fortress
A defensive setup the stronger side can't break through, despite extra material.
Hanging
A piece that can be captured for free.
Hole
A square that can no longer be guarded by a pawn, useful for enemy pieces.
Hope chess
Moving without checking whether the opponent has a reply you can't meet (Dan Heisman's term).
Initiative
The ability to make threats that force the opponent to react.
Isolated pawn
A pawn with no friendly pawns on adjacent files.
Key squares
Squares that guarantee promotion if the attacking king reaches them.
LPDO
"Loose pieces drop off" (John Nunn): undefended pieces are the usual targets of tactics.
Lucena position
The standard winning rook-and-pawn versus rook position; won by "building a bridge."
Luft
An escape square made for the king by moving a pawn in its shelter.
Minority attack
Advancing fewer pawns against more on one wing to create weaknesses there.
Opposition
Kings facing each other with one square between; the side not to move holds it.
Outpost
A square in enemy territory, protected by your pawn, that no enemy pawn can attack.
Overloading
Exploiting a piece that has too many defensive jobs.
Passed pawn
A pawn with no enemy pawns in front of it on its file or adjacent files.
Pawn break
A pawn advance that challenges enemy pawns to open lines.
Pawn chain
A diagonal line of pawns protecting each other. Attack its base.
Perpetual check
An endless series of checks that forces a draw.
Philidor position
The standard drawing method in rook-and-pawn versus rook.
Pin
An attack on a piece that can't move without exposing a more valuable piece behind it.
Prophylaxis
Preventing the opponent's plan before it happens.
Rank
A row of squares, 1 through 8.
Relative pin
A pin against a piece other than the king; moving is legal but costly.
Rule of the square
A quick test of whether a king can catch a passed pawn.
Skewer
An attack on a valuable piece that, when it moves, exposes a piece behind it.
Smothered mate
Knight checkmate against a king surrounded by its own pieces.
Space
Territory controlled behind your pawns.
Stalemate
The side to move has no legal move and isn't in check: a draw.
Static advantage
A lasting edge, like better structure or an outpost.
Tempo
A unit of time: one move. Gaining a tempo means making progress while forcing the opponent to respond.
Zugzwang
A position where any move worsens your position; you'd rather pass.
Zwischenzug
An in-between move, usually a check or threat, inserted before an expected recapture.
