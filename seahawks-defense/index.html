The Dark Side, diagrammed

How NFL defenses decide who rushes, who covers and who is lying about it — and why Mike Macdonald's Seahawks do all three better than anybody.

That little animation is the whole idea of this guide in two beats. Before the snap, the quarterback sees two safeties sitting deep — a shell that usually means the middle of the field is open. At the snap, one safety races to the middle and the other drops down toward the line. The coverage he read isn't the coverage he's facing, and he has maybe two and a half seconds to figure that out.

As of late September 2026, Seattle is the defending champion. The 2025 Seahawks allowed the fewest points in the NFL (17.2 a game), then smothered New England 29–13 in Super Bowl LX, and opened 2026 by beating the Patriots again and blowing out Arizona 31–7. The defense adopted the nickname "The Dark Side" to build its own identity apart from the Legion of Boom. This guide explains the machinery underneath: how a defense is organized, how it reads the offense, how defenders pass receivers to each other, how calls get from the sideline to eleven people in a loud stadium, and what you can watch for on TV to tell what's coming.

The language of defense

2The field through a defense's eyes

A fan watches the ball. A defense watches structure: where the offense has put its players, and what that arrangement makes possible. Before any call can make sense, you need five ideas.

The box

The box is the rectangle roughly from tackle to tackle (or out to the tight end) and about five to seven yards deep. Defenders in the box are primarily run defenders. The single most important number in run defense is how many defenders are in the box versus how many blockers the offense has there. You'll hear "six-man box," "seven-man box," "loaded box." More on that math in Chapter 6.

Strength

Before every snap, the defense declares one side of the formation "strong." The rule varies by team and by call — it might be the tight end's side, the side with more receivers, or the wide side of the field — but everyone has to agree, because every assignment is written relative to strength. A linebacker yelling "right, right!" or pointing is usually setting the strength. If the offense shifts or motions and the strength flips, the defense has to re-declare, and that is exactly the chaos offenses are trying to create.

Numbering receivers

Coverage rules never say "cover the tight end." They say "you have #2." Receivers are counted from the sideline in on each side: the widest is #1, the next is #2, then #3. A tight end with one receiver outside him is #2; if a running back swings out, he may become #3. Numbering lets one rule work against every formation the offense can invent.

Gaps

The spaces between offensive linemen are gaps, lettered from the center outward: the A-gap between center and guard, the B-gap between guard and tackle, the C-gap outside the tackle (or between tackle and tight end), and the D-gap outside the tight end. There's one of each on both sides. Run defense is, at bottom, a promise that every gap belongs to somebody.

Techniques

Defensive linemen describe where they line up with a number called a technique. A 0-technique is head-up on the center. A 1-technique shades the center's shoulder. A 3-technique sits on the outside shoulder of a guard; a 5-technique on the outside shoulder of a tackle; a 9-technique outside the tight end. An "i" means the inside shoulder, so a 4i sits on the inside shade of a tackle. Numbering varies a little between coaching trees, but these are close to universal.

Why does alignment matter so much? Because it tells a lineman which gap he owns and which blocker he's attacking. A 3-technique is built to shoot the B-gap and punish a guard who can't move him. A 1-technique makes the center's life miserable and clogs the A-gap. When fans say a team "moved Leonard Williams inside," that's a technique change, and it changes what the offensive line has to do on every snap.

In Seattle

Macdonald's fronts are built to hide which four players will rush. Linebackers "mug" the A-gaps (stand right on the line in them), edge players align in two-point stances like linebackers, and interior linemen shift between techniques. The offensive line has to decide who to block before the snap, and the front is designed to make that decision wrong.

3The eleven

Seattle lives in a nickel defense — four down linemen or edge rushers, two linebackers and five defensive backs — so that's the base picture for this guide. Tap any green defender for what the position does and who plays it for the 2026 Seahawks.

The front: linemen and edges

The interior linemen win with power and quickness in a phone booth. Their first job on a run is to own a gap and, ideally, to demand two blockers. Their job on a pass is to collapse the pocket from the inside, which is what makes quarterbacks uncomfortable fastest because they can't step up. Edge rushers win with speed and bend around the tackle. On runs, the edge player is usually the one who has to "set the edge" — keep the ball from bouncing outside him.

In a 3-4 vocabulary (which Macdonald's system grew out of), the edges are called outside linebackers and one of them is the Rush, the premier pass rusher. That's why Seattle's depth chart lists DeMarcus Lawrence as the Rush end.

The second level: linebackers

The MIKE (middle linebacker) is the quarterback of the defense. He usually wears the helmet with the radio, sets the front and strength, and makes run and pass checks. The WILL (weak-side linebacker) is typically the faster one who flows to the ball and covers backs. The SAM, the strong-side linebacker of a base defense, is often replaced by the nickel back in sub packages.

The back end: corners, nickel and safeties

Cornerbacks cover the outside receivers. The nickel (fifth defensive back) covers the slot and increasingly acts as a hybrid linebacker, blitzer and run defender. Safeties are the last line: in two-deep coverages they split the field in halves; in single-high coverages one plays the deep middle and the other comes down into the box. Modern safeties are often labeled by side ("field" and "boundary") rather than free and strong, because they flip roles constantly.

In Seattle

The nickel is where Seattle's defense is most unusual. Nick Emmanwori, a 6-foot-3, 220-pound safety who ran a 4.38 at the combine, was a Defensive Rookie of the Year finalist in 2025 playing a "big nickel" role: slot defender on one snap, linebacker on the next, pass rusher on the one after. Devon Witherspoon, nominally a cornerback, also kicks inside and was the blitzing star of Super Bowl LX. Having two defenders who can play the slot, the box and the edge is what lets the rest of the scheme work.

Defensive packages are named for the number of defensive backs. Base has four (a 4-3 or 3-4). Nickel has five, dime has six, and quarter or "dollar" packages have seven for desperation passing downs. A big nickel uses a third safety as the fifth DB instead of a smaller slot corner, which gives you a player who can hold up against the run and still cover.

The cat-and-mouse is simple to state: offenses want to put in big personnel and throw against a slow base defense, or put in small personnel and run against a light nickel. Defenses want to match: heavy against heavy, light against light. The whole league has drifted toward nickel as the true base defense because offenses spend most of their snaps in 11 personnel.

In Seattle

Seattle takes this further than anyone. Midway through 2025 the Seahawks led the NFL in nickel usage by almost ten percentage points (about 87%), and across the full season they stayed in sub packages about 91% of the time, lining up in a base 3-4 on only about 6% of snaps, the lowest rate in the league. The reason they can do it is the big nickel: when an offense brings in a second tight end to punish a small defense, the "small" defender is a 220-pound safety who fits the run like a linebacker. That also makes Seattle close to "sub-proof" against hurry-up offenses that try to trap a defense in the wrong package.

Part II

Stopping the run

5Fronts

A front is the alignment of the players near the line: which techniques the linemen play and where the linebackers stack. Fronts come in two broad families. Even fronts put four players on the line with a gap in the middle (no one on the center's nose). Odd fronts put someone head-up or shaded on the center, often with five players across the line.

Within the even family, the two classics are Over and Under. In an Over front, the 3-technique goes to the strength of the formation and the nose shades to the weak side. In an Under front, it's flipped: the 3-technique goes weak and the nose shades strong. Each creates a different "bubble" — an uncovered offensive lineman who can release to block a linebacker — and puts a different linebacker in charge of the strong-side gaps.

The odd family's modern star is the Tite (or "Mint") front: two 4i-techniques and a 0-technique, which squeeze the inside gaps closed and dare the offense to run outside, where fast edge players and safeties are waiting. It's the signature front of the Vic Fangio family that Macdonald's Baltimore system sits beside.

You don't need to memorize these. The point is that every front is a trade: it gives the offense one kind of run and takes away another. That's why fronts shift and move right before the snap, and why a lot of "pressure" is really just a front that moves late so the offensive line's blocking rules are pointed at the wrong people.

6Run fits and the math of the box

A run fit is each defender's assignment against the run: which gap he's responsible for, and what he does when a blocker comes at him. The principle is gap integrity. If every gap has exactly one defender and nobody abandons his gap, a running back has nowhere to go. Most long runs happen because one player guessed.

Box math and the plus-one

Count the offense's blockers in the box (five linemen plus any tight ends or fullbacks) and count the defenders. If the offense has six blockers and the defense has seven defenders, the defense has a "plus-one" — one defender who can be unblocked and make the tackle. The classic way to get that seventh or eighth defender is to drop a safety into the box, which leaves only one safety deep: a single-high coverage like Cover 1 or Cover 3.

The quarterback also counts. Because the QB doesn't block, a defense technically has an extra player against a handoff. Offenses reclaim that player with read options and RPOs (run-pass options) that make one defender wrong whether he plays the run or the pass. Modern run defense is largely about who handles that conflict.

Spill, box and the alley

When a pulling guard or a lead blocker comes at an edge defender, he has a choice built into the call: spill the play (hit the blocker's inside shoulder to force the ball to bounce outside, where fast pursuit is waiting) or box/force it (keep his outside arm free and turn the ball back inside to the linebackers). The alley is the lane between the edge player and the next defender outside; safeties and nickel backs "fit the alley" from depth.

That diagram is the core of how two-high defenses survive. On paper, a six-man box against six blockers is even, and even is bad for the defense. But if the two interior linemen each draw a double team, two defenders have occupied four blockers. Now the offense is short, the linebackers can flow freely, and the safety arrives from depth as the extra man. The safety is "late," but he's also unblocked — offensive linemen can't get to a player who starts 13 yards away.

In Seattle

Before the Super Bowl, Kyle Shanahan, whose 49ers had failed to score a touchdown against Seattle in their last two meetings, described the problem precisely: Seattle stays in a two-deep look all day, and you can't normally stop the run that way, but they do. The reason is up front. Leonard Williams and Byron Murphy II draw double teams, so the Seahawks can play with a six-man box and let the nickel and WILL linebacker arrive late as leverage players. Macdonald's shorthand for the philosophy is "Stop the run, have some fun": kill the run early, force obvious passing downs, and then unleash the pressure package. In Super Bowl LX the Patriots had 33 rushing yards on 11 carries at halftime.

Part III

Coverage

7Man, zone and the space between

Every pass coverage answers two questions: how many defenders are deep, and is each underneath defender responsible for a person or a place?

In man coverage, a defender's rule is a player: "you have #2 wherever he goes." It's tight and aggressive, but defenders turn their backs to the quarterback to chase their man, so scrambles and crossing routes hurt. In zone coverage, the rule is a place: "you have the flat," "you have the deep third." Defenders see the quarterback and break on the ball, but receivers can sit in the soft spots between zones. Match coverage (pattern-matching) is the modern hybrid: it starts as zone and turns into man based on how the routes unfold. Almost every NFL zone call today has some match rules inside it.

Middle of the field: open or closed

Coaches sort coverages into two families by what happens in the deep middle. If one safety sits in the middle, the middle of the field is closed (MOFC, "single-high"): Cover 1 and Cover 3. If the two safeties split the field and nobody's in the middle, it's open (MOFO, "two-high" or "split-field"): Cover 2, Cover 4, Cover 6 and their relatives. This is the first thing a quarterback checks before the snap and the first thing you should check too. It's also exactly what disguise attacks.

The numbers in coverage names mostly describe the deep defenders: Cover 2 has two deep halves, Cover 3 has three deep thirds, Cover 4 has four deep quarters. Cover 1 is man coverage with one deep safety; Cover 0 is man with nobody deep. Cover 6 is half Cover 4 and half Cover 2 (four plus two). Flip through all eight below. Each opens on the look the offense sees before the snap, then shows where everyone goes.

Seattle is a split-field team first. Its most distinctive call is Cover 6: in 2025 the Seahawks led the NFL in Cover 6 usage at roughly 23% of snaps, per analyst Cody Alexander's charting. 8Pattern matching: how defenders hand off receivers

Here's the problem with pure zone: four vertical routes against Cover 3 put two receivers in the middle deep third, and one safety can't cover both. Here's the problem with pure man: crossing routes and stacked receivers make defenders run into each other. Pattern matching solves both by giving defenders rules that say, in effect, "play your zone until a route declares itself, then lock onto the receiver who threatens it, and hand him off when he leaves."

The vocabulary of a handoff

  • Carry: follow a vertical route up the field until a deeper defender can take it. A hook defender might carry a seam to 12 yards.
  • Wall: get in front of a receiver who's trying to cross your face and force him to take a longer path. Linebackers wall the tight end off the line.
  • Sink: drop with depth under a deep route to take away the throw behind you.
  • Pass / zone off: release a crossing receiver to the next defender in his path ("cross!") rather than chasing him across the field.
  • Read #2: the key for many split-field defenders. What the second receiver does tells the safety whether he's playing man on #2, helping on #1 or fitting the run.
  • Banjo / in-out: two defenders against two stacked or bunched receivers; the inside defender takes whoever breaks in first, the outside defender takes whoever breaks out.

Some teams use names from Nick Saban's Cover 3 match system, where a corner in MOD ("man only deep") plays man if #1 goes vertical but passes off short routes, and MEG ("man everywhere he goes") plays true man on him. The labels differ by team, but the logic is universal: react to the route distribution, not just the zone.

Quarters: the safety reads #2

Quarters is the purest example. The safety aligns at 10–12 yards and reads the #2 receiver (on the right here, the tight end). If #2 goes vertical, the safety takes him deep, essentially man coverage. If #2 goes flat or blocks, the safety is freed to help the corner on #1, or to hammer the run. Flip between the two tabs.

Zone off the crosser

Crossing routes are the engine of modern NFL offenses because they're poison for man coverage: a defender chasing across the field gets picked or outrun. In zone and match coverages, linebackers pass crossers from one to the next with a loud call. The first defender walls the route and carries it to the edge of his area, then yells "cross!" while the next defender takes over. The first defender sinks back to his spot to look for the next route coming in behind.

Banjo against stacks and bunches

Offenses stack receivers one behind the other, or bunch three in a cluster, specifically to make man defenders collide and to make zone defenders argue about who has whom. The answer is a banjo: decide who breaks which way, not who lined up where. The two tabs below use the same stack; only the route breaks change, and so do the assignments.

In Seattle

This is where continuity shows up. Defensive coordinator Aden Durde has said the back end became far more connected in 2025, and that entering 2026 the whole defense understands why each teammate is doing what he's doing, not just their own job. Ernest Jones IV described the next level as anticipation: knowing where the soft spots are in a given coverage and getting there to cover for them. Those are descriptions of pattern-matching mastery. 9Disguise and rotation

A quarterback's pre-snap read tells him three things: whether the middle is open or closed, where the pressure might come from, and which side to work. Every one of those answers gets him a head start. Disguise takes the head start away.

The basic tool is rotation: showing one shell and moving to another at the snap. The terms describe who rotates where. In a sky rotation the safety comes down to the flat; in a buzz rotation a safety drops down to the hook/curl area; in a cloud the corner takes the flat and a safety takes the deep half over him. Spin usually describes one safety rotating to the deep middle while the other drops into a robber spot. Rotations can go to the strength or away from it.

Timing is everything. Rotate too early and the quarterback just reads the new shell. Rotate too late and a safety is still traveling when the ball is thrown, which is a big play waiting to happen. Elite disguise teams start moving as the center's hands tighten on the ball or as the quarterback's eyes drop to the snap, so the picture changes in the exact instant he stops looking.

The Fangio family trick

The two-high revolution that started around 2020 with Vic Fangio's defenses (and his protégé Brandon Staley) isn't all quarters, which is a common misunderstanding. The system uses depth as a disguise tool and still plays a lot of Cover 3, rotated away from the strength. Fangio's split-field call "8" (Cover 2 to the passing strength with a poach safety on the backside) and his weak Cover 3 call "9" look identical at the snap. Cover 6 in that tree is called "Stuff," typically used to get two defenders on a lone receiver. As offenses have gotten heavier, two-high coverage hasn't gone away: charting services put the league's split-field rate somewhere between 36% and 43% in 2025.

In Seattle

Going into Super Bowl LX, New England had drilled film of Seattle's late rotations and drops that punished quarterbacks for trusting their first read, and it didn't matter. Seattle's secret is how the coverages sync with the front: the rotation behind the line is married to a stunt or pressure in front of it. In the 2026 opener, Macdonald leaned on Cover 6 on about 41% of snaps against Drake Maye, who in 2025 led the league in interceptions, sacks and scrambles against split-field coverage. Maye saw it on 21 dropbacks and produced two interceptions and three scrambles. That's a game plan built on one quarterback's weakness.

Part IV

Pressure

10Four-man rush, blitz and everything between

A blitz, strictly speaking, is any rush of five or more. But the most interesting pressure in the modern NFL is the stuff that isn't a blitz at all. Here's the full menu.

  • Four-man rush: the four linemen rush, seven drop. The baseline.
  • Stunt (also "game" or "twist"): the four rushers exchange gaps. One lineman slants to occupy a blocker, another loops behind him into the space that opens.
  • Simulated pressure ("sim"), or creeper: still only four rushers, but not the four the offense expected. A linebacker or nickel rushes and a lineman drops into coverage. The offense's protection is built to block the obvious four, so an unexpected rusher often comes free, while the defense still has seven in coverage.
  • Fire zone: a five-man pressure with three deep and three under zone defenders behind it. It's safer than a man blitz because nobody is on an island.
  • Man blitz: five or six rushers with man coverage behind, often Cover 1 or Cover 0. High risk, high reward.
  • Green dog (or "hug"): a man-coverage defender whose assigned back stays in to block rushes instead, so the blocker is occupied and the defender adds pressure.
  • Mug looks: linebackers stand in the A-gaps before the snap threatening to rush. Sometimes they do. Often they bail.

The stunt

The most common game is the T-E stunt ("tackle-end"): the defensive tackle crashes outside into the offensive tackle, and the end loops inside behind him into the gap the guard just vacated. If the guard and tackle don't pass the stunt between them cleanly, the looper runs free.

Simulated pressure

The sim is the heart of Macdonald's scheme. Look at what the offense is shown before the snap: two linebackers mugged in the A-gaps, meaning as many as six could rush. The protection has to account for them. Then at the snap one mugged backer rushes, the nickel comes off the edge, and a lineman and an edge player drop out. It's still a four-man rush with seven in coverage, but the protection slid toward the wrong people.

A five-man pressure with rotation behind it

When Seattle does send five, the coverage behind it usually rotates. Here the nickel blitzes from the slot, and the safety on that side rotates down to take the area the nickel vacated while the other safety spins to the deep middle. That's the family of nickel pressures Macdonald ran repeatedly in the Super Bowl.

In Seattle

The numbers describe a sniper, not a machine gun. In 2025 Seattle blitzed only about 22% of the time, yet ranked third in the NFL in pressure rate (40.1%). It was third in stunt rate at roughly a quarter of snaps. Analyst Cody Alexander calls it "passive pressure": the structure and compression of the defense matter more than the volume of the blitz package. When the Seahawks do send extra rushers it tends to hit. Through eight weeks of 2025 they were the only team that hadn't allowed a touchdown pass when blitzing, and opponents completed just 53.3% of their passes against Seattle pressure. The one situation where Seattle really leans on the blitz is third-and-long, at around 40%. Film analysts have documented pressure families with names like "Pick," "Chucky" and "Palms" in Seattle's tape; whatever the real names are, the pattern is the same: pressure paths aimed at the B-gaps, where modern protections are thinnest.

11Protections and the quarterback's clock

To understand pressure, you have to know what it's attacking. Pass protections come in a few flavors:

  • Slide: the line blocks zones, sliding one direction as a unit. The running back takes the first threat on the opposite side. Great against stunts on the slide side, vulnerable to overloads on the back side.
  • Man / "big on big": each lineman takes a specific defender; backs take linebackers. Clear rules, but a stunt or a late-moving linebacker can confuse who is whose.
  • Half-slide: part man, part slide, the most common NFL answer.
  • Max protect: keep the tight end and back in to block, sending only two or three receivers. Good against blitzes, but the defense can drop eight into coverage.

The center usually sets the protection by identifying the MIKE ("54 is the mike!"). Watch for that call — it tells you who the offense thinks is the most dangerous second-level player. Defenses that swap linebackers late, walk safeties down or mug the A-gaps are specifically trying to make that identification wrong.

If more defenders rush than the offense can block, somebody is hot: the quarterback has to throw immediately to a receiver who adjusts his route (a "sight adjustment") into the space the blitzer vacated. That's why a blitzing defense wants the vacated space to be filled by someone else, which is exactly what the rotation in the previous diagram does.

The clock

Most NFL pass plays are designed around a clock of roughly two and a half to three seconds. Pressure doesn't need to produce a sack to win; it only needs to make the quarterback throw before the route develops or to a place the defense chose. A checkdown to a running back for three yards on third-and-eight is a defensive win.

In Seattle

Seattle's defense seems built around that idea. In 2025 it ranked third in the league in checkdown rate (about 14%), and when it sent pressure it held opponents to around four yards per dropback regardless of the coverage behind it. As film analysts have put it, receivers were "open" exactly where the coverage wanted the ball to go: low, short of the sticks and in front of a secondary that tackles well.

Part V

The operation

12Reading keys: what each defender watches

Defenders don't watch the ball. Each has a key, a specific player or area whose first movement tells him what the play is. The key comes from his position and the call.

  • Defensive linemen read the blocker in front of them. If the lineman pops up ("high hat"), it's probably a pass. If he fires out low ("low hat") or drives laterally, it's a run, and the angle of his helmet tells the direction.
  • Linebackers usually read the guards and the running back's path together. A guard pulling across the formation says "gap scheme, follow him." All linemen stepping the same way says "zone." A guard setting back says "pass; get to your drop."
  • Safeties often read #2 (as in quarters) or the "triangle": the nearest offensive tackle, the back and the quarterback.
  • Cornerbacks read the #1 receiver's release and, in zone, the quarterback's drop. A three-step drop means the ball is coming out quickly; a play-action fake means eyes back to your receiver immediately.

Offenses attack keys. Play-action shows run keys (low hats, a back taking a handoff) so linebackers and safeties step forward, then throws behind them. RPOs leave one defender unblocked and let the quarterback decide after the snap whether that defender played the run or the pass. Bootlegs send the flow one way and the quarterback the other. Every one of these is a bet that a defender will trust his key a split-second too long.

13Communication: from the headset to the snap

A defensive call has to get from the play-caller's mouth to eleven players, get adjusted to whatever the offense shows, and survive crowd noise, all in about 25 seconds. Here's the chain.

The call

In Seattle, head coach Mike Macdonald calls the defense himself. Defensive coordinator Aden Durde's role, as he's described it, is to take weight off the play-caller: see around corners, handle the areas he's strongest in, and support the call rather than make it. Macdonald has framed it as a team effort where he's simply the one hitting the button.

The call goes through the coach-to-player radio to one defender wearing a helmet marked with a green dot. Most teams give that helmet to the middle linebacker, which in Seattle's lineup is Ernest Jones IV. The radio cuts off automatically when the play clock hits 15 seconds, so anything the defense needs to know after that has to happen on the field.

A defensive call typically stacks three pieces: the front (alignment and strength rule), any movement or pressure (a stunt, a sim, a blitz), and the coverage. A made-up example in that shape would be "Over, Tex, Six": Over front, a tackle-end stunt, Cover 6. Many defensive players also wear a wristband with a numbered call card so a short number can stand in for a long call.

On the field

The green-dot linebacker relays the call in the huddle or with signals. Once the offense breaks the huddle and shows its formation, the second wave of communication starts:

  • The linebackers declare the strength and any front adjustment to the linemen.
  • The safeties confirm the coverage and any checks with the corners and nickel. Against certain formations, a call might "check" automatically (for example, from quarters to a cloud coverage against a trips formation).
  • If the offense motions, someone makes the adjustment call: "lock" or "travel" (man; follow him), "bump" (zone; everyone slides), or a rotation change.
  • After the snap, route calls fly: "cross!", "wheel!", "under!", "switch!" These are the handoffs from Chapter 8, spoken aloud.

Much of this is nonverbal: hand signals, pointing, eye contact. It has to be, because a home crowd can drown out the defense too.

In Seattle

Lumen Field's noise is famous for wrecking opposing offenses, but Leonard Williams has noted that it also complicates Seattle's own defensive communication, so the team works on verbal and nonverbal systems specifically for home games. Durde listed communicating through crowd noise as a training-camp emphasis heading into 2026. Continuity is the advantage: with 10 of 11 Super Bowl starters back in Year 3, players can operate on what Durde calls an unspoken understanding. Jones added that the group can now take more into a game mentally and do more with it.

14Personnel rotation and substitution

Between plays, a coach on the sidelines watches which offensive players are running onto the field and signals the defensive personnel group. If the offense substitutes, the officials must give the defense a chance to substitute too; that's why you'll see an official stand over the ball. Offenses counter with tempo: stay in the same personnel and snap quickly, trapping the defense in whatever package it had on the field. That's the value of a package, like Seattle's big nickel, that is reasonable against both heavy and light groups.

Defensive linemen rotate constantly because pass rushing is exhausting. Many teams rotate by series or by a snap count; top rushers often play 60–75% of snaps, and specialists come in for passing downs. Seattle's 2026 front is deep enough to rotate freely: Leonard Williams, Byron Murphy II, Jarran Reed and second-year Rylie Mills inside; DeMarcus Lawrence, Uchenna Nwosu, Derick Hall and veteran Dante Fowler Jr. on the edges. In the Super Bowl, ten different Seahawks recorded a pressure, and Mills, who had played one playoff snap before the game, got a sack.

Rotation also happens within a play call. A defense might have one player who aligns as a linebacker on first down and an edge rusher on third down, which is why depth charts for defenses like Seattle's can be misleading: Derick Hall is listed as a backup but plays like a starter.

15Situational football

The same defense plays very differently depending on down, distance, field position and the clock. The call sheet is organized by situation, not by play.

  • First and ten: the offense can do anything, so defenses play sound, balanced calls built to stop the run. Seattle's blitz rate is lowest on early downs.
  • Second and long: offenses often run to "stay on schedule" or throw screens. Defenses can mix in pressure and disguise.
  • Third and short (1–3 yards): heavier fronts, tighter man coverage, run-stopping first.
  • Third and medium (4–7): the home of 2-Man, sim pressures and match coverages that defend the sticks.
  • Third and long (8+): the defense can sit deep and rally, or blitz to force a quick throw short of the marker. Seattle blitzed about 40% of the time here in 2025.
  • Red zone: the field shrinks, so deep zones become end-zone zones. Expect more man, more brackets, and more aggressive underneath defenders because nothing can get behind them.
  • Two-minute: keep everything in front, tackle in bounds, force the offense to use timeouts. Rushing three and dropping eight is common here.
  • Backed up / goal line: heavy personnel, everyone crowding the line.

Coaches also call "sequences": showing a look early in a game specifically so they can run something different out of it later. A pressure a defense has shown three times becomes a perfect disguise for a coverage-only call on the fourth.

In Seattle

Colleagues describe Macdonald as coaching defense the way offensive coaches coach offense: plays with specific roles for every player, a heavy emphasis on situations, and disguises and pressures that can come from anywhere. In the Super Bowl he flipped his usual script, blitzing about 33% of the time in the first half and only 8% in the second, after the lead forced New England to throw into his coverage rotations.

Part VI

The Seahawks way

16Where this defense comes from

Macdonald is a product of the Baltimore Ravens' in-house coaching pipeline, one of the most prolific in the league. He started there as an intern in 2014, became an assistant, coached defensive backs, and took over as linebackers coach in 2018 when Wink Martindale became coordinator. In 2021 he left to be Jim Harbaugh's defensive coordinator at Michigan, where the defense went from 116th in one advanced efficiency ranking to 12th in a single year.

In 2022 John Harbaugh brought him back to Baltimore to replace Martindale, whose defense had slid to 28th in DVOA and leaned heavily on blitzing and man coverage. Macdonald streamlined the calls, rewrote the teaching and moved the defense toward zone and disguised pressure. By 2023 the Ravens led the NFL with 60 sacks and finished first in DVOA. Analysts sometimes call this the "Ravens 2.0" system.

Seattle hired him in 2024 as the league's youngest head coach. The pattern repeated: the defense struggled early in Year 1, then climbed from 20th to 11th in EPA per play over the second half, and in Year 2 became the best scoring defense in football: 17.2 points a game, 4.4 yards per play allowed, 47 sacks and 18 interceptions in the regular season, and seven more takeaways in the playoffs.

17Seven things Seattle does differently

1. Two-high shell, light box, run still dies

Most teams that play two deep safeties give up rushing yards and accept it. Seattle doesn't, because its interior linemen command double teams and its nickel and WILL arrive late as extra run defenders. This is the complaint Shanahan made before the Super Bowl, and it's the foundation for everything else.

2. Nickel is base

Roughly 91% of Seattle's 2025 snaps came in sub packages, with the base 3-4 on about 6%. The big nickel is why they can.

3. Pressure without blitzing

Third in pressure rate on one of the league's lowest blitz rates. Stunts, sims and front movement do the work that other teams need a sixth rusher for.

4. The sniper blitz

When the blitz comes, it's timed to the situation (third-and-long) or aimed at a specific weakness found on film. Devon Witherspoon explained after Super Bowl LX that the staff had found tells in how New England's guards and tackles set, including whether they would overset against certain rushes, and built the blitzes around them.

5. Pressure married to rotation

Almost every pressure comes with a disguise or rotation behind it, which is why quarterbacks under Seattle pressure check the ball down rather than find a hot receiver running free.

6. Game-plan specificity

Seattle doesn't play one defense every week. It played Cover 6 on 41% of snaps against Drake Maye in the 2026 opener because Maye struggled against split-field coverage, far above its already league-leading season rate. It blitzed a third of the time in the first half of the Super Bowl because it had found protection tells. The menu is the same; the order changes by opponent.

7. Fundamentals under the complexity

None of the disguise works if players miss tackles or blow assignments. Seattle's defensive backs tackle well enough to let short completions die, and continuity gives the unit the shared understanding to execute complex calls fast. Jones has said the team's standard is perfection: it doesn't want to give up a yard, and it gets angry when it does.

18Legion of Boom vs. the Dark Side

Seattle has now had two historically great defenses in one generation, and they could hardly be more different. Pete Carroll's teams led the league in scoring defense four straight years (2012–2015) with a system famous for its simplicity; Macdonald's won it in 2025 with a system famous for its complexity.

20Film room: two games against the same quarterback

Super Bowl LX: Seattle 29, New England 13

Seattle came out swinging. It blitzed about 33% of the time in the first half, far above its norm, and over the game Macdonald dialed up six variations of a nickel blitz with Witherspoon as the missile. Nothing in the plan was exotic; it attacked the soft spot in the Patriots' B-gaps and the tells Witherspoon described. At halftime Drake Maye was 6-of-11 for 48 yards, had been sacked three times, and had completed just two of five throws beyond five yards. New England had 33 rushing yards. The score was only 9–0, but the game was over structurally.

In the second half, with the Patriots forced to throw, Seattle's blitz rate dropped to about 8% and the coverage rotations took over. The knockout came with 4:27 left: Witherspoon screamed off the edge on another nickel blitz, jarred the ball loose as Maye threw, and Uchenna Nwosu intercepted the loose ball and returned it 45 yards for a touchdown. Final tally: six sacks by four different players, three takeaways, ten players with a pressure, six with a pass breakup, and a shutout through three quarters. The defense was still annoyed about two late touchdowns.

2026 Week 1: Seattle 13, New England 10

The rematch was a completely different game plan. Instead of blitzing, Seattle sat in split-field coverage, playing Cover 6 on about 41% of snaps. New England stayed conservative and led 10–3 entering the fourth quarter, with Seattle down to backup quarterback Drew Lock after Sam Darnold's hip injury on the opening drive.

Then the coverage started winning. On the second play of the fourth quarter, Nehemiah Pritchett intercepted a deep shot to Romeo Doubs, setting up Lock's 45-yard touchdown to Jaxon Smith-Njigba. On the next Patriots drive, Julian Love read a throw over the middle to DeMario Douglas and picked it, setting up the go-ahead field goal. And with 26 seconds left, facing third-and-5 at the Seattle 16, Maye was flushed from the pocket and forced a ball into the end zone that Josh Jobe intercepted. Three interceptions in one quarter, the first time Seattle had done that since Richard Sherman and Earl Thomas did it in 2016.

Same opponent, same quarterback, opposite plans: pressure-heavy in February, coverage-heavy in September. That flexibility is the scheme.

Part VII

Watching the game

21The pre-snap scan

The broadcast gives you a few seconds of the formation before the snap, often from a high angle. Here's the scan, in the order that gives you the most information fastest. With practice it takes about as long as the play clock's last ten seconds.

  1. Count the defensive backs.Five DBs is nickel, six is dime. If a big safety is playing the slot, that's Seattle's big nickel, and it means the defense isn't worried about the offense's size.
  2. Count the safeties deep.One in the middle at 12–15 yards: closed middle (Cover 1 or 3). Two split at 12–15 yards: open middle (2, 4, 6). Then keep watching, because this is what disguise changes.
  3. Check safety depth and width.Wide on the hashes and deep (14+) suggests Cover 2 halves. Tighter and shallower (10–12) suggests quarters, where safeties need to fit the run. One safety creeping toward the box late suggests a rotation to single-high.
  4. Look at the corners.Pressed, inside leverage, eyes on the receiver: man. Off at 7+ yards, eyes on the quarterback: zone. Squatting at 5 yards with outside leverage: Cover 2 or a cloud corner in Cover 6. A corner who's shallower on one side than the other is a clue about which side is the "half" side.
  5. Find the nickel.Is he over the slot, inside the slot (hinting at a blitz path), or walked into the box like a linebacker?
  6. Check the linebackers.Normal depth is about 5 yards. Linebackers standing in the A-gaps are a mug look. Linebackers creeping forward as the clock runs down may be coming, or may be selling it.
  7. Count the box.Six versus seven or eight tells you how much the defense respects the run and whether it has a safety to spare.
  8. Look at the line's stances.Edge rushers in wide alignments with weight forward are pass-rush alignments. A defensive tackle who has shifted from a 3-technique to a 4i may be setting up a stunt.
  9. Check down and distance.Third-and-long changes everything. Seattle's blitz rate roughly doubles there.
  10. Watch the snap, not the ball.The last half-second before the snap is where the defense shows its real hand. Safeties that start moving as the center grips the ball are rotating.
What to watch for

The single best habit: when the broadcast shows the pre-snap look, pick a safety and watch only him at the snap. Where he goes in the first two steps tells you the coverage family better than anything else on the field.

22Motion: the offense's truth serum

Offenses use pre-snap motion partly to answer the question "man or zone?" If a receiver motions across the formation and a defender runs with him, that's almost always man coverage. If the defenders instead shuffle over a few steps each ("bump"), passing responsibility along, it's zone or match. Watch both tabs.

Defenses know this, of course. Good defenses use "lock and bump" mixes, where the motion receiver's defender travels only part of the way before passing him off, or where a man coverage uses a safety to take the motion man so the corner doesn't have to cross the formation. Motion also forces the defense to re-declare strength, which is when miscommunication happens. If you see two defenders pointing at each other after a motion, something is about to go wrong or right.

What to watch for

Motion right at the snap ("jet" or "orbit" motion) is a different weapon: it's designed to change the run fit and stress the defenders who have to chase it. Watch whether Seattle's safeties or nickel react to it, or whether the defense lets it go and trusts its structure.

23The first two seconds after the snap

If you missed the pre-snap look, the first two seconds still tell you almost everything. Here's where to look, in order.

  • The safeties' first steps. Both backpedal and widen: two-high, likely Cover 2 or 6. Both backpedal but stay tight, reading: quarters. One sprints to the middle and one drives down: rotation to Cover 3 or Cover 1.
  • The corners' hips. A corner who turns and runs with the receiver from the line is in man or deep-third coverage. A corner who sits and keeps his eyes in the backfield is playing the flat, which means Cover 2 or a cloud side.
  • The linebackers' depth. If the MIKE sprints straight back 15 yards to the middle of the field, it's Tampa 2. If linebackers are running with backs or tight ends, it's man.
  • Count the rushers. Four or five? Did a linebacker or nickel rush while a lineman dropped out? That's a sim. Replays from the end-zone angle are perfect for this.
  • Where the quarterback looks. Quarterbacks usually look first where the pre-snap read told them to. If he pumps and then checks down, the rotation probably took away his first read.
What to watch for in Seattle games

Watch the defensive lineman aligned over the guard on obvious passing downs. If he drops into coverage, you're watching a sim. Watch Witherspoon and Emmanwori before the snap: where they align inside or outside the slot hints at a blitz path. And watch the checkdowns. When the opposing quarterback dumps it short on third down and the running back is tackled immediately, that's the design working.
